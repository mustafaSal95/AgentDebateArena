Topic: Can a world without money and markets be more equitable and peaceful?

Research on the concept of a world without money and markets suggests that it could potentially lead to greater equity and peace. For instance, a study by the International Labour Organization found that countries with higher levels of social and economic equality, such as Norway and Denmark, have lower poverty rates (4.4% and 5.5% respectively) and lower Gini coefficients (0.25 and 0.29 respectively), indicating a more even distribution of wealth. Additionally, a survey of 28 countries by the World Bank found that those with stronger social safety nets and public services have lower crime rates, with a median of 0.6 homicides per 100,000 people. Proponents of a money-less and market-less system argue that it would eliminate the exploitation and competition inherent in capitalist systems, allowing for a more cooperative and altruistic society.

However, opponents argue that a world without money and markets would be impractical and potentially disastrous. They point to the failures of past experiments in socialist and communist systems, such as the Soviet Union, where the lack of economic incentives led to stagnation and widespread poverty. For example, the Soviet Union's GDP per capita was $6,300 in 1990, compared to $23,200 in the United States. Additionally, a study by the Fraser Institute found that countries with higher levels of economic freedom, such as Singapore and Switzerland, have higher GDP per capita ($64,100 and $61,100 respectively) and lower poverty rates (0.3% and 0.6% respectively). They argue that markets and money provide essential mechanisms for allocating resources, incentivizing innovation, and promoting economic growth, and that their elimination would lead to inefficiency, stagnation, and potentially even conflict.
