Topic: Should the death penalty be abolished globally?

The debate on abolishing the death penalty globally is a contentious issue, with proponents arguing that it is a violation of human rights and opponents claiming it is a necessary deterrent. According to Amnesty International, 106 countries have abolished the death penalty, while 54 countries still retain it. In 2020, 483 executions were carried out in 18 countries, with China, Iran, and the United States being among the top executioners. The United States, in particular, has seen a decline in death penalty sentences, with 17 executions in 2020, down from 98 in 1999. Those in favor of abolition argue that the death penalty is often applied arbitrarily, with 72% of all executions in 2020 being carried out in countries with poor human rights records.

Key arguments against the death penalty include its lack of deterrent effect, with studies showing that homicide rates are not significantly lower in countries with the death penalty. For example, a 2019 study found that the homicide rate in the United States was 4.88 per 100,000 people, compared to 0.85 per 100,000 in Canada, which abolished the death penalty in 1976. Additionally, the risk of executing innocent people is a significant concern, with 167 people exonerated from death row in the United States since 1973. On the other hand, proponents of the death penalty argue that it is a necessary tool for justice, particularly in cases of heinous crimes. They also argue that life imprisonment can be more expensive than the death penalty, with a 2019 study estimating that the average cost of a death penalty case in the United States is $620,932, compared to $618,034 for a life sentence. However, these arguments are countered by the fact that the death penalty is often applied disproportionately to marginalized groups, with 42% of those on death row in the United States being African American, despite making up only 13% of the population.
