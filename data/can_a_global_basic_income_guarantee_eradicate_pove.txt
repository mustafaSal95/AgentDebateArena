Topic: Can a global basic income guarantee eradicate poverty?

Implementing a global basic income guarantee (GBIG) has been proposed as a potential solution to eradicate poverty. Proponents argue that a GBIG could provide a safety net for the 736 million people living in extreme poverty, defined as less than $1.90 per day. According to the World Bank, poverty rates have declined from 35% in 1990 to 9% in 2015, but progress has been uneven and 413 million people still live on less than $3.20 per day. A GBIG could help address this issue, with estimates suggesting that a monthly stipend of $100 could lift 470 million people out of extreme poverty. For example, the Alaska Permanent Fund has provided an annual dividend to residents since 1982, resulting in a 20% reduction in poverty rates.

However, critics argue that a GBIG may not be a feasible or effective solution to eradicate poverty. They point out that the estimated annual cost of a GBIG, ranging from $3.9 trillion to $4.3 trillion, would require significant funding and redistribution of resources. Additionally, a GBIG may not address the root causes of poverty, such as lack of education, job opportunities, and access to healthcare. For instance, a study in Finland found that a two-year basic income experiment had no significant impact on employment rates or well-being. Furthermore, implementing a GBIG would require international cooperation and agreement on funding, implementation, and targeting, which could be challenging to achieve. Despite these challenges, some experts argue that a GBIG could be a necessary step towards achieving the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goal 1, which aims to eradicate poverty by 2030.
