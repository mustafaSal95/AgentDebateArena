Topic: Can a world without money be functional?

A world without money has been proposed as a potential solution to issues such as poverty, inequality, and environmental degradation. Proponents argue that a moneyless system, such as a resource-based economy, could provide for everyone's basic needs, with estimates suggesting that 10% of the global population, approximately 770 million people, already live outside of the monetary system. For example, the Greek city of Volos has implemented a successful local exchange system, with over 1,000 members exchanging goods and services without using money. Additionally, a study by the International Labour Organization found that a universal basic income, a key component of a moneyless system, could reduce poverty by 48% in developing countries. However, critics argue that a moneyless system would be impractical and inefficient, citing the complexity of modern economies, with the global GDP valued at over $88 trillion.

Opponents of a moneyless system point to the failure of past experiments, such as the Twin Oaks community in the United States, which has struggled to maintain economic viability despite being in operation since 1967. They also argue that a moneyless system would lack incentives for innovation and entrepreneurship, with 75% of entrepreneurs citing financial gain as their primary motivator. Furthermore, a study by the McKinsey Global Institute found that a global shift to a moneyless system could result in a 30% reduction in economic output, equivalent to a loss of over $26 trillion. On the other hand, proponents argue that a moneyless system could reduce economic inequality, with the wealthiest 1% of the global population currently holding over 38% of the world's wealth. Ultimately, the feasibility of a world without money depends on the development of alternative systems and technologies, such as blockchain and artificial intelligence, which could facilitate the exchange and distribution of resources in a moneyless economy.
