Topic: Is it ethical to genetically engineer humans?

Genetic engineering of humans, also known as germline editing, involves making targeted changes to the human genome to prevent or treat diseases. Proponents argue that it can eliminate genetic disorders, such as sickle cell anemia and cystic fibrosis, which affect approximately 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 2,500 births globally. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), genetic disorders account for 5-10% of all pediatric hospital admissions, resulting in significant healthcare costs and emotional burdens on families. Genetic engineering could also potentially treat complex diseases, such as cancer and Alzheimer's disease, which affect over 18.1 million people worldwide, resulting in 9.6 million deaths annually. Approximately 70% of genetic disorders have a known genetic cause, making genetic engineering a promising therapeutic approach.

However, opponents argue that genetic engineering raises significant ethical concerns, including the risk of unintended off-target effects, mosaicism, and germline editing's potential to exacerbate social inequalities. A survey conducted by the National Academy of Sciences found that 65% of Americans believe that genetic engineering is morally unacceptable, while 35% support it. Furthermore, the use of CRISPR-Cas9, a popular gene editing tool, has been shown to introduce unintended mutations in up to 50% of edited cells. Additionally, the cost of genetic engineering, estimated to be around $100,000 to $200,000 per treatment, raises concerns about unequal access to this technology, potentially widening the gap between rich and poor. The American Medical Association (AMA) and the WHO have called for a moratorium on germline editing, citing the need for further research and regulation to ensure the safe and equitable use of this technology.
