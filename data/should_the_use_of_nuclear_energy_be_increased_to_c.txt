Topic: Should the use of nuclear energy be increased to combat climate change?

The use of nuclear energy to combat climate change is a highly debated topic. Proponents argue that nuclear energy is a low-carbon source of electricity, with the World Health Organization (WHO) estimating that it prevents 1.8 million air pollution-related deaths annually. The International Energy Agency (IEA) reports that nuclear energy currently generates 10% of the world's electricity, with 440 operational reactors worldwide. Increasing nuclear energy production could significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions, with the Nuclear Energy Institute (NEI) estimating that a 20% increase in global nuclear capacity by 2050 could prevent 2.5 gigatons of CO2 emissions annually. Furthermore, the IEA estimates that nuclear energy can reduce CO2 emissions from the energy sector by 45% by 2050, meeting 17% of global electricity demand.

However, opponents argue that nuclear energy poses significant environmental and health risks, including the storage and disposal of radioactive waste. The World Nuclear Association reports that there are currently 270,000 tons of nuclear waste stored globally, with an additional 10,000 tons generated annually. The cost of nuclear energy production is also a concern, with the IEA estimating that the average cost of building a new nuclear reactor is $6,500 per kilowatt of capacity. Additionally, accidents such as Fukushima (2011) and Chernobyl (1986) have highlighted the catastrophic consequences of nuclear energy production, with estimated costs of $200 billion and $235 billion, respectively. The European Union's climate and energy framework aims to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 80% by 2050, and while nuclear energy can contribute to this goal, its role must be carefully weighed against the risks and costs associated with its production.
