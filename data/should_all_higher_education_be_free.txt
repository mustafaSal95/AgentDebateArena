Topic: Should all higher education be free?

Research indicates that implementing free higher education can have significant benefits, including increased enrollment rates and improved social mobility. In countries where higher education is free, such as Germany and Norway, enrollment rates are substantially higher, with 94% and 83% of students pursuing higher education, respectively. Additionally, a study by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) found that countries with low or no tuition fees have higher levels of educational attainment, with an average of 43% of adults holding a tertiary degree. Proponents of free higher education argue that the benefits outweigh the costs, citing the long-term economic gains of a more educated workforce, with estimates suggesting that every dollar invested in higher education generates a return of $1.50 in economic growth.

However, opponents of free higher education argue that the costs are prohibitively expensive, with estimates suggesting that implementing free higher education in the United States would require an additional $70 billion in annual funding. They also argue that free higher education can lead to inefficiencies and abuse, with 40% of students in Germany dropping out of university without completing a degree. Furthermore, a study by the Federal Reserve found that student loan debt in the US has increased by 450% since 2003, but the default rate is relatively low, at 11.5%. Opponents argue that means-tested subsidies and income-driven repayment plans can provide more targeted support to students who need it most, rather than providing a blanket subsidy to all students, regardless of their financial situation. Ultimately, the debate surrounding free higher education is complex, with valid arguments on both sides, and policymakers must carefully weigh the costs and benefits of such a policy.
