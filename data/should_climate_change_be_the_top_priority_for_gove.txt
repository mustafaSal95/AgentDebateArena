Topic: Should climate change be the top priority for governments worldwide?

Climate change is a pressing global issue, with the past decade being the warmest on record, according to NASA and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The average global temperature has risen by 1.1°C since the late 19th century, with 2020 being 1.02°C above the 1880-1900 average. Proponents of prioritizing climate change argue that immediate action is necessary to mitigate its devastating impacts, including rising sea levels, more frequent natural disasters, and loss of biodiversity. The economic costs of inaction are substantial, with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimating that climate change could lead to a 7% loss in global GDP by 2100. In contrast, investing in renewable energy and reducing greenhouse gas emissions could create up to 24 million new jobs globally by 2030, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA).

However, opponents argue that prioritizing climate change may divert resources away from other pressing issues, such as poverty, hunger, and access to healthcare. For example, the United Nations estimates that 736 million people still live in extreme poverty, and 820 million people suffer from hunger. Additionally, the World Health Organization (WHO) reports that 400 million people lack access to essential healthcare services. Governments may need to balance competing priorities, allocating limited resources effectively. Nevertheless, many experts argue that climate change exacerbates these existing issues, with the World Bank estimating that climate change could push up to 143 million people into poverty by 2050. Ultimately, the decision to prioritize climate change depends on weighing the potential benefits of immediate action against the potential costs and competing demands on government resources.
